**Dan Davis** works out of western Ohio, has been nominated for two Eisner
awards, and is not related to the other Davis who draws a cat. He has inked for
DC — *Batman: The Brave and the Bold*, *Scooby-Doo* — and for Bongo on *The
Simpsons* and *Radioactive Man*. He has drawn Garfield strips, and since 2017 he
has been the sole artist on *Crankshaft*. Lately he has been making his own book,
*All Luck Comics*.

His site describes the job in one sentence: he collaborated as an inker, using
brush and pen, to draw the final line art over a penciller's work.

I have had this link on a page for fifteen years and I think it is the most
underrated thing on the list, because mainstream comics is the most explicit
division of labour in any creative industry, and almost nobody outside it knows
the roles exist.

## The pipeline

A single page of a superhero comic typically passes through five pairs of hands.
A writer produces a script. A penciller turns it into drawn pages. An inker
produces the final line art over those pencils. A colourist works over the inks.
A letterer sets the dialogue and the sound effects. Then it is printed, on a
monthly schedule that does not move.

This is a production line, with specialised stations, work-in-progress inventory,
and a takt time. The reason it exists is the reason any production line exists:
monthly serialised publishing is too much work for one person at the required
rate, so the work was decomposed into tasks that different people can do
simultaneously on different issues.

What makes it interesting rather than merely industrial is the interface.

## Inking is not tracing

The naive view is that the penciller draws it and the inker goes over the lines,
which would make the inker a human plotter. That is not the job. Pencils arrive
in wildly different states — some pencillers deliver near-finished work, others
deliver loose indications and expect the inker to resolve them. The inker decides
line weight, which is what creates depth and focus on a flat page; decides what
is rendered in black and what is left for the colourist; and quietly fixes
anatomy that does not work.

So the handoff is not a file transfer. It is an interface with a negotiated
tolerance, and the tolerance is different for every pairing. Two people who have
worked together for years operate with a loose, high-trust interface and move
fast. A new pairing needs tighter specification and produces more rework. The
same page can be better or worse depending entirely on who hands what to whom.

Every engineer who has managed a handoff between two teams recognises this
exactly. We write interface control documents precisely because the tolerance
cannot be left implicit, and we all know that the document is never the real
agreement — the real agreement is how much each side trusts the other to catch
their mistakes.

Comics has been running this experiment monthly since the 1930s, under deadline,
at scale, with the results published and attributed. The credits box on page one
tells you who did which stage. I cannot think of another industry that documents
its division of labour that legibly, that publicly, every single month.

There is a last detail I like. Davis spent a career finishing other people's
lines, and is now writing and drawing his own book. The man at the station in
the middle of someone else's pipeline went and built his own.
