The link on my page says **Red Silas**. Click it today and you land on
`csneal.com`, under the name Christopher Silas Neal.

The redirect is clean and the old name is almost gone, but not quite: the contact
address at the bottom of the site is still `chris@redsilas.com`. Fifteen years of
business cards and invoices are hanging off that domain, and he is clearly not
going to be the one to break them.

## Two names for the same person

A studio name is a hedge.

It lets a freelancer look like a company, which matters when the person
commissioning you is a procurement department that cannot raise a purchase order
for a human being. It lets you grow without rebranding. It puts a layer between
your work and your self, which is useful in a business where the work is rejected
often and the rejection is not personal but certainly feels it.

Dropping it is the opposite move. Your own name on the door means the work and
the person are the same thing now, and there is nowhere to stand when it goes
badly. People usually make that change at a particular moment — when the work has
become distinctive enough that the name is the asset, and hiding it behind a
studio is costing more than it protects.

Neal's trajectory makes the moment legible. He started in editorial: magazine and
newspaper illustration, fast turnarounds, somebody else's article, work that is
on a train floor by Tuesday. Then book covers. Then, in 2009, Chronicle asked him
to illustrate a non-fiction picture book by Kate Messner called *Over and Under
the Snow* — they wanted a non-traditional artist. It came out in 2011, was a *New
York Times* Editor's Choice, and took an E. B. White Honor the following year. By
2015 he was writing his own, starting with *Everyone*.

Editorial work is anonymous by construction. Picture books are not: your name is
on the cover, next to the author's, and the book stays in print for twenty years.
Somewhere on that path `redsilas.com` stopped being a useful place to hide.

## The part that is an engineering story anyway

His process, briefly, because it is the most quietly technical thing on this list:
he draws in pencil, builds a digital mock-up, then paints each colour separately
in traditional media, and assembles the painted elements in Photoshop.

He is not painting a picture. He is producing separations — the same logic as a
screenprint or a four-colour press, where the image does not exist on any single
plate and only appears at assembly. The decision to work that way buys him
physical texture, which you cannot fake convincingly, and digital revision, which
you cannot do on a finished painting. It costs him the ability to judge the whole
thing until late.

That is a process-planning decision with a clear tradeoff, made once, and then
lived with for a career. I have read worse justifications in manufacturing
reports.
