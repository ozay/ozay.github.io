My list spells him **Ray Ceasar**. It is Caesar. The typo has been sitting there
for fifteen years and I am going to leave it, because a link list is a document
of what you knew at the time.

Ray Caesar makes portraits — girls in interiors, pale, composed, dressed for a
century that is not this one, wrong in a way you cannot immediately locate. They
look like paintings. They are not. They are rendered in **Maya**, the 3D
animation software, and that fact is the whole reason this entry has outlasted
most of the others on my list.

## The tool and its intended industry

Maya was built so that studios could animate films. It is a modelling,
rigging, lighting and rendering pipeline for moving pictures made by large teams
on deadlines.

Caesar uses it to produce single still images, alone, slowly.

He builds each figure as a surface model, gives it an articulated skeleton so he
can pose it, paints some textures by hand in Photoshop and sources others
photographically, renders the components separately and composites something on
the order of sixty or seventy layers into one picture. That is a production
pipeline with a bill of materials. It is also, functionally, a painting.

He did not learn any of this at art school. He spent roughly seventeen years in
the art and photography department of Toronto's Hospital for Sick Children,
making medical and technical illustrations — documentation of surgical
reconstruction, of psychology cases, of injuries to children. Then, between 1998
and 2001, he worked as a senior animator at a Toronto visual effects house, which
is where the software came from.

So the sequence is: a career spent drawing damaged bodies accurately for
institutional purposes, then a tool for making imaginary bodies move, then thirty
years of still images about damaged bodies that are not accurate about anything
except the feeling.

## Why this is the companion to the 3D printing post

I have written here before about reading an article on 3D printing in 2006 and
what caught me: a process where complexity is free, which inverts a rule I had
been taught to design around.

Caesar is the same reversal in software. Maya's economics assume you need
twenty-four frames per second and a crew. If you only need one frame and you are
one person, every constraint the tool was built around stops applying, and the
budget it was designed to spread across a film can be spent on a single image.
Seventy composited layers for one picture is an absurd allocation in animation.
For one picture it is just thorough.

The general version, which I think is the most reliable creative heuristic there
is: find a tool built for an industry with different economics from yours, and
apply it at the wrong scale. Most of what looks like originality is somebody
doing exactly this.

The uncomfortable part of his story is that it is not really about software. It
is about a man who looked at the worst things that happen to children, as a job,
for seventeen years, and then spent the rest of his working life making pictures
of children who are beautiful and composed and slightly wrong. The tool is the
interesting half. The other half is not a design lesson.
