Two entries on my list have come apart in opposite directions, and putting them
next to each other says something I have not been able to shake.

## The dead man whose site is immaculate

**Antonio Saura** was born in Huesca in 1930 and died in Cuenca in 1998. He was
largely self-taught, having spent a long stretch of his adolescence bedridden
with tuberculosis. In 1957 he co-founded El Paso, the group that dragged Spanish
painting into contact with post-war abstraction under a dictatorship that was not
enthusiastic, and he led it until it dissolved in 1960. He painted crucifixions
for decades, in open argument with the Velázquez in the Prado.

His site is still up, and it is not a portfolio. Nobody is touting for work.
The navigation tells you exactly what it is for: the foundation, the catalogues
raisonnés, **authentication**, **restoration**, **rights**. The copyright line
reads *© succession antonio saura*.

That is not a website about an artist. It is the public interface of an estate —
the apparatus that decides whether a painting that turns up at auction is really
his, that controls who may reproduce what, that maintains the definitive list
against which every claim is checked. It will outlive everyone currently
operating it, because it has to. A catalogue raisonné is a permanent institution
with a website attached, not the other way round.

## The living illustrator whose site is gone

**Fernanda Cohen** is the second link on my list. The domain no longer resolves —
not a timeout, not a parking page, just no answer from any nameserver.

As far as I can tell nothing has happened to Cohen. Trained at the School of
Visual Arts in New York, moved back to Buenos Aires around 2011, pen and ink and
gouache, work for *The New Yorker*, Coca-Cola, Gap. Presumably still drawing,
somewhere, at an address I do not have.

Nobody died. A domain renewal simply stopped going through, probably years ago,
possibly at a move, and fifteen years of links pointing at that name — mine among
them — quietly became links to nothing.

## What actually survives

The pattern across my whole list is not about quality, effort or fame. It is
about whether there is an institution behind the address.

The agency's site survives because a company with employees renews it. TOCA ME's
site survives because a studio and a regional funding body are attached to it.
Saura's site survives because an estate has a legal obligation to maintain the
record. Simon Oxley's site survives, for now, because somebody is still paying
for it — and the moment that stops, the sentence *Hello, I am Simon* goes with it.

The personal domains are the fragile ones. A personal domain is not a possession;
it is a tenancy, renewed annually, by one person, forever.

This should worry academics more than it does. Our institutional pages die the
moment we change jobs — I have watched colleagues' entire publication records
evaporate in a departmental site migration. The things that genuinely persist are
the ones with an institution and an identifier behind them: the DOI, the ORCID
record, the thesis in the national archive. Those are the Saura model. A personal
site is the Cohen model, and I say that as someone running one.

I am not going to stop. But I have stopped pretending it is permanent. The right
posture for a personal site is a tenancy taken seriously: keep the thing small,
keep it portable, make sure anything that matters also exists somewhere with a
registrar you are not personally responsible for.

And leave the dead links in. They are the only evidence that something was once
there.
