The name on my link is the older one. The firm was founded in 1977 by Steve

stopped using — which is the sort of thing a fifteen-year-old list is good for.

It is the least glamorous entry here and probably the most important, because it
is the only one that explains how the other eleven eat.

## What a rep actually does

An illustration agency is not a gallery and not a talent show. It is a set of
answers to problems a freelancer is badly placed to solve alone:

**Discovery.** An art director with a Thursday deadline does not want to search.
They want twelve plausible options by lunchtime from someone who has already
filtered for availability and fit.

**Pricing.** This is the real service. An individual illustrator, asked what they
charge for "a cover," has no basis for an answer — they see one or two of these
negotiations a year. An agency sees several a week, across a hundred artists, and
therefore actually knows what the market pays, which rights are normally included,
and what an unusual request is worth. It is an information asymmetry, and the
agency exists on the correct side of it.

**Rights.** What is being sold is almost never the drawing. It is a licence:
territory, medium, duration, exclusivity. Each of those is a lever, and each one
is a thing a tired freelancer gives away for free when the client says "we'll need
it for everything."

**Collections.** Somebody has to chase the invoice. It should not be the person
who has to work with that client again.

## Why I keep coming back to this one

Go back to the first post in this series. Simon Oxley put the Twitter bird on a
stock library and received a few dollars for an image that went on to anchor a
company's identity. Nobody cheated him. There was simply no institution standing
between the drawing and the transaction, and so there was nobody whose job it was
to ask the second question: *and what if this becomes the logo?*

That is exactly the question an agency is for. The Octocat shows the other branch
of the same tree — GitHub came back, negotiated exclusive rights, and did it
properly, because a logo needs a licence that a stock download does not provide.
The difference between the two outcomes is not talent and not luck. It is whether
there was a professional intermediary in the room.

We teach supply chain students to be suspicious of middlemen, and then spend the
rest of the course explaining what distributors, brokers and agents actually do:
aggregate information, carry risk, smooth transactions, enforce terms. An
illustration agency takes twenty-five or thirty percent and is, on this evidence,
underpriced.
