The first link on a page I built about fifteen years ago is **I Do Kung Foo**. It
is the studio name of Simon Oxley, a British illustrator who worked for years in
Japan and later from the countryside near Oxford, and who drew two things you have
looked at thousands of times without knowing whose hand made them.

He drew the original Twitter bird. He drew the cat with octopus tentacles that
became GitHub's Octocat.

## The part that belongs in an engineering course

He made almost nothing from either.

The bird went onto iStockphoto in 2006, one of thousands of drawings he uploaded —
he once described his own output as coming off a sausage factory line. Someone at
Twitter bought it for somewhere between ten and fifteen dollars. After the library
took its commission his share was a few dollars. He did not know the company was
using it until 2009, when he saw his own bird on a CNN broadcast.

The Octocat started the same way and ended differently. He uploaded it in 2007 as
*Octopuss*. GitHub found it while looking for something to put on a 404 page, and
because the stock licence did not permit use as a logo, they came back, negotiated
exclusive rights and did it properly.

Two nearly identical drawings, two completely different outcomes, and the
difference has nothing to do with the drawings.

This is why I think the story belongs in a course on product development rather
than a conversation about art. We are comfortable teaching students that price
should bear some relationship to cost, or to value. Here is a mark that went on to
anchor the identity of a company worth billions, bought for less than lunch. Not
because anyone was cheated — the transaction was exactly what both parties agreed
to — but because a per-transaction market has no mechanism for pricing what a
thing might later become. The stock library sold a file. The value was created
somewhere downstream, years later, by people who had never heard of him.

Every procurement system I have taught has this hole in it somewhere. We price the
deliverable. We have no column for the option.

## Checking a link

I went back through this list recently to see what had survived. Most of it has;
these pages are more durable than you would expect.

Simon Oxley died on 4 April 2026, aged 56. The notice is on his Dribbble account,
written by his family: thirty years of doing what he loved, from mascots and logos
for all sorts of companies to small models to decorate the house, starting as a
boy drawing every night in his room. They thanked everyone who had ever left a
like on his work, because he read them.

His own site is still up and still in the present tense. *Hello, I am Simon.
Designer of graphics and character mascots.* Then the list — the bird, the
Octocat, Sammy the Shark, the Bitly puffer fish, the old iStockphoto logo.

I had this link on a page for fifteen years and never knew the name attached to
it. That seems worth a post, and worth the nine that follow.
