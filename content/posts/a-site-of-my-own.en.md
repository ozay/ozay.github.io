For fourteen years I have been adding rows to lists. A row for each course, a row
for each paper, a row for each thesis. The lists grew, they stayed accurate, and
at some point they stopped being readable by anyone who was not already me.

That is the honest reason this site exists in its new form. Not a redesign for
its own sake — a recognition that a list is a bad answer to most of the questions
people actually arrive with.

## The questions people actually ask

Three groups of people find their way here, and they want different things.

**Students** want to know what a course is like before they register, and whether
I have taught it before. The old answer was a line of text reading
`Previous: 2018-2019 Fall, 2019-2020 Fall, 2020-2021 Fall…`. The new answer is a
course explorer you can filter by level, language, institution and theme — and
each course shows the terms it has actually run, so you can see which ones are
load-bearing and which were tried once.

**Colleagues and reviewers** want to know what I work on, not what I have
published. Those are different questions. A chronological publication list answers
the second and hides the first. So the research page now leads with the shape of
the work — publications per year, themes, who I write with — and the list comes
after, filterable, with the co-author network drawn properly as a network.

**Prospective supervisees** want to see finished theses. Nine of them are listed,
with their YÖK archive IDs, so they can be read rather than taken on trust.

## What I changed my mind about

I used to think the job of an academic website was completeness. Put everything
on it, keep it current, let people search. I now think completeness is the floor,
not the ceiling. The same fourteen publications arranged two different ways are
two different documents, and only one of them tells you anything.

The other thing: it is bilingual now, properly, not as a translated afterthought.
About half of what I teach is in Turkish. A site that only spoke English was
quietly misrepresenting the work.

## The boring details

It is a static site — plain HTML, CSS and a small amount of JavaScript, no build
step, no framework, no analytics, no cookies. The publications, courses and theses
live in three data files I can edit in a text editor, which means updating the site
each semester is a two-minute job rather than a weekend one. That was the real
design constraint. A site you dread updating is a site that goes stale.

If something here is wrong — a course term I have mis-recorded, a co-author's name
misspelled — I would genuinely like to know.
