In 2006 I read an article in *Popular Mechanics* about 3D printing. That is the
whole inciting incident. I spent the rest of that day reading about the topic
instead of doing what I was supposed to be doing, and then I kept reading.

What caught me was not the novelty. It was a particular reversal. In every
manufacturing process I had studied, complexity costs money: an extra feature means
an extra operation, an extra tool, an extra setup. Here was a process where a
complicated part and a simple part of the same volume cost roughly the same to
make. That is not an improvement to manufacturing. It is a different set of rules,
and it means the design decisions you were trained to make are the wrong ones.

That reversal is why the topic belongs in a product development course rather than
a materials one. If complexity is free, "what should this part look like?" stops
being a question about what the machine can cut.

## From an article to a laboratory

Years later I turned it into a research project at Doğuş University and was granted
a sum to buy equipment: a **Formlabs Form 2** and an **Ultimaker 2+ Extended**,
along with resin and filament. One stereolithography machine and one fused
deposition machine — deliberately, because the point was never to own a printer. It
was to let students hold two parts made by two different logics and work out why
one of them has a visible grain and the other does not.

## What I would tell 2006

Mostly that the hype cycle was going to be exhausting and that it would not matter.
The consumer "a printer in every home" story collapsed, as it deserved to. What
survived is the thing that was interesting in the first place: short runs, awkward
geometries, parts you need four of rather than forty thousand of, and the medical
and aerospace cases where a part fits exactly one person or one airframe.

Nineteen years on, the article was right about the mechanism and wrong about the
market. I think that is the usual ratio, and it is still worth losing a day to.
