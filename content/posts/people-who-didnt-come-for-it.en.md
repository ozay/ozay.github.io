The last two links on my list are the odd ones out. Neither is a portfolio.
Both are attempts to explain a subject to people who did not turn up wanting to
learn it, and that is a harder problem than it sounds.

## A thesis project that escaped

**Startups, This Is How Design Works** went online in April 2012. Wells Riley
made it as his capstone project for a graphic design degree at Northeastern,
after a stretch consulting for Boston startups and finding that the founders he
worked with had no vocabulary for what he did. Not hostility — absence. They
could not say what they wanted, could not say why they disliked a thing, and
could not tell the difference between a disagreement about taste and a
disagreement about function.

So he wrote the missing briefing: what design is, why it has value, enough
history to place it, and enough vocabulary to argue with a designer productively.
One page, free, no login. It is still up fourteen years later, which for a
student project is remarkable, and the reason is that it answered a question
people were actually asking and nobody had written down.

Riley went on to found Hack Design and to work as a product designer. The page
has outlived the circumstances that produced it.

## A bank that explains itself

**Osmanlı Bankası Müzesi** sits on Bankalar Caddesi in Karaköy, in the Ottoman
Bank's former headquarters — Alexandre Vallaury's building, opened in 1892 on the
street Evliya Çelebi had known as Voyvoda Yolu and which became, in the second
half of the nineteenth century, the financial centre of the empire. The museum is
arranged in and around the safe-deposit vaults.

In 2011 Garanti folded its cultural institutions together into SALT, and the
Ottoman Bank Archives and Research Centre became part of SALT Galata, whose
research library now runs to tens of thousands of volumes and periodical titles —
including the *Annuaire Oriental*, the commercial yearbooks that let you find out
who was trading from which building in which year.

What the museum does is the same move as Riley's page, performed on a bank
archive. A ledger is not interesting. A ledger explained — this is what a clerk
earned, this is what the building cost, this is who banked here and what happened
to their money when the empire ended — is extremely interesting, and the
difference is entirely in the framing. The audience is a person who walked in off
a street in Karaköy because the door was open.

## The thing they have in common

Neither has a captive audience.

Riley's reader can close the tab. The museum's visitor can walk back out onto
Bankalar Caddesi. Nobody is being assessed, nobody needs the credit, and the
moment either one becomes boring, the audience is gone and does not come back.
Both are therefore forced into a discipline that university teaching mostly
avoids: you must earn the next thirty seconds, continuously, from first contact
to the end.

I teach with a captive audience. My students are assessed on whether they
absorbed the material; they cannot leave; the incentive to stay is external and
strong. That is an enormous hidden subsidy and it lets us get away with a great
deal — with lectures that bury the interesting thing on slide forty, with
material organised by the logic of the discipline rather than by what a newcomer
can actually hold, with the assumption that motivation is somebody else's
problem.

The useful exercise is to ask of any lecture: if these people could leave, when
would they? Usually the honest answer is early, and usually the fix is the one
both of these sites made — lead with the thing that is actually interesting, give
people the vocabulary before the content, and respect the fact that nobody owes
you their attention.

That is the list. Twelve links I saved fifteen years ago and had not looked at
properly since: eleven still standing, one gone, one person dead, two names
changed, and rather more about my own field than I expected to find in a page of
illustrators.

I would not have predicted any of that from the bookmarks. It seems worth
keeping the habit — and worth going back through the list occasionally, which
turns out to be the part I had never done.
