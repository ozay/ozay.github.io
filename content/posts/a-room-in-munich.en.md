**TOCA ME** is Spanish for "touch me," which is a strange name for a design
studio in Munich. It was founded there in 2003 by Nina Steenackers and Thorsten
and Ron Iberl, and the thing it is actually known for started the following year:
a design conference that mixed talks, an exhibition and a club night and drew
more than five hundred people on its first outing.

Twenty-odd years later it is still running. It moved from the Reithalle to the
Alte Kongresshalle, sold out completely in 2019, ran inside Munich Creative
Business Week in 2022, and the most recent edition the site documents is 2023.
Somewhere north of a hundred artists have spoken — Joshua Davis, Jessica Walsh,
Erik Kessels, Seb Lester, Malika Favre, Yuko Shimizu, Annie Atkins. It is
part-funded by bayern design and the Bavarian economics ministry, which is to say
a regional government decided this was infrastructure.

## A field organised around a room

What interests me is the form, not the line-up.

My field organises itself around journals and proceedings. The unit of
professional exchange is a paper: written alone or in a small group, reviewed
anonymously, archived permanently, citable, indexed, and — importantly —
consumable by someone who was not there. Conferences exist, but the conference is
largely a delivery mechanism for the papers, and the papers are the durable
artefact.

Design organises itself around the room. The unit is a talk, often undocumented,
frequently about process rather than result, given once to the people who showed
up. There is no archive worth the name. There is no review. You cannot cite it.
Twenty years of TOCA ME exists mainly as photographs of a stage, a community
page, and whatever the eight hundred people in the hall took away with them.

Each form preserves what the other throws out.

We keep the claim and lose the practice. A paper records what was concluded, not
how anybody actually works, and our methods sections are famously a genre of
fiction. Design keeps the practice and loses the claim: a TOCA ME talk will show
you the forty discarded versions and the argument with the client, and then
vanish, and nothing in it is checkable by anyone who was not in the room.

I would not trade ours for theirs. But I notice that when I want to learn how
someone actually does the work — the sequence, the dead ends, the thing they do
that they have never written down — I do not go to a journal. I go and find a
recording of a talk.

## The fossil layer

One honest detail. The site has a section on artworks on the blockchain, NFTs,
left over from the period when every design organisation in Europe had one.

I like that they have not deleted it. A site that has been running for twenty
years should have strata, and that is a perfectly legible 2021. My own list has
the same property — that is the entire premise of this series — and the rule I am
applying to my typo of Ray Caesar's name applies to their NFTs: leave it. The
record of what you believed is worth more than the appearance of having always
been right.
