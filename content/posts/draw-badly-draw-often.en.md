**Jon Burgerman** took a first in fine art from Nottingham Trent in 2001, moved
to New York in 2010, and has spent the quarter-century since producing doodles at
a volume that makes people slightly uncomfortable. Characters, scribbles, murals,
colouring books, a book of creative exercises. His work is in the Victoria and
Albert and in Nottingham Castle, which is a funny place for a doodle to end up.

He is usually described as the leading figure in doodle art, which makes it sound
like a movement. It is better understood as a position on how often you should be
allowed to be wrong.

## The cost of one attempt

Here is the thing worth stealing.

Burgerman's practice is built so that a single attempt costs almost nothing. A
pen, a surface, a few minutes, no setup, no approval, no materials budget, no
irreversibility. The consequence is that he can afford to make something bad —
and because he can afford it, he does it constantly, and because he does it
constantly he is enormously good at it.

That is not a statement about talent. It is a statement about unit cost. He has
engineered his process so the marginal cost of an experiment rounds to zero, and
everything else follows from that one decision.

Now look at how we usually run a design course. The project is large. There is one
deliverable. It is assessed once, at the end. Materials are limited, the printer
queue is three days long, and a failed attempt costs a student a week they do not
have. We have built a process where the marginal cost of an experiment is very
high — and then we are puzzled when students converge early on a safe idea and
defend it past the point of sense.

They are behaving correctly. Under those costs, conservatism is the rational
strategy. The students are not timid; the process is expensive.

## What I would change

Not the brief. The economics.

Make the first twenty attempts cost nothing: no grade, no materials, no showing
them to anyone, no file to submit. Make the deliverable a selection from a pile
rather than a single object carried from week one. Judge the pile sometimes, so
that volume is visibly worth something.

This is well-trodden ground — it is most of what prototyping literature has been
saying for thirty years — but it is easier to argue from Burgerman than from the
literature, because he is standing there as the finished result of the argument.
Someone who drew badly, often, in public, for twenty-five years, and is now in
the V&A.

The version that sticks with students is blunter: you are not going to think your
way to a good idea. You are going to make forty bad ones and notice which one
you keep coming back to.
