Second on the list is **Adrian Johnson**, a British illustrator who worked for
years from the south coast of England and now works from Copenhagen. If you have
used the London Underground, read the *Guardian* or the *New York Times*, or
looked at an Adidas or Volkswagen campaign, you have probably seen his work
without registering it as anyone's.

His drawings are flat. Not flat as in dull — flat as in no gradients, no
rendered shadow, no attempt to convince you there is a light source. A small
number of colours, laid down as areas, with the shapes doing all of the work.

## Subtraction is the expensive part

The thing I find useful about this work is not the style. It is what the style
costs him.

Every gradient he does not use is a decision he made once and does not have to
make again. The palette is settled before the brief arrives, so the question
"what colour should this be?" has four possible answers instead of sixteen
million. There is no rendering, so there is no argument about how much rendering.
The constraints are not decoration. They are a specification, written in advance,
and they are what lets the work be fast and consistent across thirty years and a
few hundred clients.

Engineers understand this in one direction and resist it in the other. We are
completely comfortable with the idea of a design standard — a preferred parts
list, a fastener family, a house tolerance — because it reduces variety downstream
and variety is where cost hides. We are much less comfortable applying it to the
part of the work we think of as creative, where "keeping our options open" still
sounds like a virtue.

It is not a virtue. An open option is a decision deferred, and a decision deferred
has to be made later, by someone with less time, under worse conditions,
inconsistently with the twelve similar decisions made by other people the same
week. Johnson's palette is the same argument as a standard parts list. It is just
drawn.

## What this looks like in a studio class

The version of this I would defend in a product development course: give the
constraint before the brief, not after.

A student handed an open brief will produce something undisciplined and then
spend the critique defending it. A student handed four colours, no gradients and
a square format will produce something, discover within an hour where the
constraint actually binds, and have something real to argue about. The constraint
does not reduce the design space in any way that matters at the beginning. It
just makes the space navigable.

Johnson has been doing this long enough that the constraint is no longer visible
as a constraint. It reads as a voice. That is usually what a voice is: a set of
decisions somebody stopped relitigating.
