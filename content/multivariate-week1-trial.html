What is a vector?

A vector has a magnitude (length) and a direction. We draw it as an arrow from a tail to a head.

Position does not matter

Two arrows with the same length and direction are the same vector.

Notation: \(\mathbf{A}\), \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) (from \(P\) to \(Q\)). Length: \(|\mathbf{A}|\). A plain number is a scalar.

The blue head changes the vector. The grey tails only move the copies.

Components

Put the tail at the origin; the head lands at a point \((a_1,a_2,a_3)\):

\[\mathbf{A}=\langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle=a_1\hat\imath+a_2\hat\jmath+a_3\hat k\]

\(\hat\imath,\hat\jmath,\hat k\) are the unit vectors along the axes.

Head minus tail

\[\overrightarrow{PQ}=\langle q_1-p_1,\;q_2-p_2,\;q_3-p_3\rangle\]

Length, by Pythagoras twice: \(|\mathbf{A}|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}\).

Drag \(P\) and \(Q\). The faint arrow at the origin is the same vector.

Adding and scaling

Algebra: componentwise.

\[\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}=\langle a_1+b_1,\,a_2+b_2,\,a_3+b_3\rangle,\qquad c\mathbf{A}=\langle ca_1,ca_2,ca_3\rangle\]

Geometry:

  • \(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\): head-to-tail, or the diagonal of the parallelogram.
  • \(c\mathbf{A}\): same direction if \(c>0\), reversed if \(c<0\), length \(|c||\mathbf{A}|\).
  • \(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}+(-\mathbf{B})\): the arrow from the head of \(\mathbf{B}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{A}\).

Unit vectors and direction

Unit vector

A vector of length 1. Every nonzero \(\mathbf{A}\) has exactly one unit vector in its direction:

\[\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{A}}{|\mathbf{A}|}\]

MIT S1, Problem 2

Find the unit vector in the direction of \(\langle 2,3\rangle\).

\(|\langle2,3\rangle|=\sqrt{13}\), so \(\mathbf{u}=\left\langle \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{13}},\tfrac{3}{\sqrt{13}}\right\rangle\).

Lengths

\(\mathbf{A}=\langle1,2\rangle,\ \mathbf{B}=\langle1,-1\rangle,\ \mathbf{C}=\hat\imath+2\hat\jmath+3\hat k\). Find \(|\mathbf{A}|\), \(|\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}|\), \(|\mathbf{C}|\).

\(|\mathbf{A}|=\sqrt5\); \(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}=\langle2,1\rangle\) so \(|\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}|=\sqrt5\) too; \(|\mathbf{C}|=\sqrt{14}\).

Magnitude and direction separate: \(\mathbf{A}=|\mathbf{A}|\,\mathbf{u}\). However far you drag \(\mathbf{A}\), \(\mathbf{u}\) stays on the unit circle.

Locations, displacements and travel distance

IE application

Positions of machines, storage bays or customers are points; a trip is a displacement vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\).

  • Straight-line travel: \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|=\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2}\) (Euclidean).
  • Aisle travel: \(|\Delta x|+|\Delta y|\) (rectilinear, “Manhattan”), the metric of most facility-layout and warehouse models.
  • A closed route: displacements add up to \(\mathbf 0\); distances add up to the route length.

Drag the depot \(D\) and the stops \(A\), \(B\).

A rower crossing a river

MIT S1, Problem 1

A river flows east at 3 km/h; a rower rows at 6 km/h relative to the water. Which heading takes the boat straight across (north)?

Set-up. Water \(\mathbf w=\langle 3,0\rangle\); rowing \(\mathbf r=6\langle\cos\theta,\sin\theta\rangle\). The actual velocity \(\mathbf w+\mathbf r\) must have zero \(x\)-component.

\[3+6\cos\theta=0\;\Rightarrow\;\cos\theta=-\tfrac12\;\Rightarrow\;\theta=120^\circ.\]

Heading \(30^\circ\) west of north; speed across \(6\sin120^\circ\approx5.2\) km/h.

(b) River 6, rower 3: \(6+3\cos\theta=0\) has no solution, so the boat cannot go straight across. Try it with the sliders.

Vectors as lists of quantities

IE application

A vector need not be an arrow in space. Any ordered list of numbers is a vector, and the same algebra applies.

  • Production plan \(\mathbf x=\langle x_1,\dots,x_n\rangle\): units of each product.
  • Price or cost vector \(\mathbf p=\langle p_1,\dots,p_n\rangle\).
  • Inventory state of a warehouse network; demand profile over 12 months.

In this course we mostly stay in 2D and 3D so we can draw; the formulas are the same in \(\mathbb R^n\), which is where Operations Research lives.

The dot product, algebraically

Dot product

\[\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3\qquad(\text{a scalar})\]

Rules (all follow from the formula):

\[\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf A,\quad \mathbf A\cdot(\mathbf B+\mathbf C)=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B+\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf C,\quad (c\mathbf A)\cdot\mathbf B=c(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)\] \[\boxed{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A=|\mathbf A|^2}\]

Examples (MIT S2)

\(\langle6,5\rangle\cdot\langle1,2\rangle=6+10=16\). \((\hat\imath+2\hat\jmath)\cdot(3\hat\imath+4\hat\jmath)=3+8=11\). \(\hat\imath\cdot\hat\jmath=0,\ \hat\imath\cdot\hat\imath=1\).

The geometric definition, and why the two agree

Key idea

\[\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=|\mathbf A|\,|\mathbf B|\cos\theta\]

\(\theta\) is the angle between the vectors, \(0\le\theta\le\pi\).

Proof sketch

  1. Law of cosines on the triangle with sides \(\mathbf A,\mathbf B,\mathbf A-\mathbf B\): \[|\mathbf A-\mathbf B|^2=|\mathbf A|^2+|\mathbf B|^2-2|\mathbf A||\mathbf B|\cos\theta.\]
  2. Expand the left side with the dot product: \[|\mathbf A-\mathbf B|^2=(\mathbf A-\mathbf B)\cdot(\mathbf A-\mathbf B)=|\mathbf A|^2-2\,\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B+|\mathbf B|^2.\]
  3. Compare the two: \(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=|\mathbf A||\mathbf B|\cos\theta\). \(\square\)

The angle is measured from the picture, the sum from the numbers. They always agree.

What the sign tells you

\[\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B}{|\mathbf A||\mathbf B|}\]
  • \(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B>0\iff\theta<90^\circ\) (acute): roughly the same direction.
  • \(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=0\iff\theta=90^\circ\): perpendicular (orthogonal).
  • \(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B<0\iff\theta>90^\circ\) (obtuse): roughly opposite.

Test for perpendicularity

\(\mathbf A\perp\mathbf B\iff\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=0\) (for nonzero vectors).

Shaded: every \(\mathbf B\) with \(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B>0\). Its edge is the line perpendicular to \(\mathbf A\).

Try these before revealing

Angle between two vectors

(a) \(\mathbf A=\langle1,2,2\rangle,\ \mathbf B=\langle2,-1,0\rangle\). (b) \(\mathbf A=\hat\imath+8\hat\jmath,\ \mathbf B=\hat\imath+2\hat\jmath\).

(a) \(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=2-2+0=0\Rightarrow\) perpendicular, \(\theta=90^\circ\).

(b) \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{1+16}{\sqrt{65}\sqrt5}\approx0.943\), so \(\theta\approx19.4^\circ\).

Unknown length (MIT S2, Problem 2)

\(\mathbf B=\langle2,2,1\rangle\), the angle between \(\mathbf A\) and \(\mathbf B\) is \(30^\circ\), and \(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=6\). Find \(|\mathbf A|\).

\(|\mathbf B|=3\), so \(6=|\mathbf A|\cdot3\cos30^\circ\Rightarrow|\mathbf A|=\dfrac{6}{3\cdot\sqrt3/2}=\dfrac4{\sqrt3}\approx2.31\).

A right triangle in space (MIT notes)

\(A=(4,3,6),\ B=(-2,0,8),\ C=(1,5,0)\). Which angle of triangle \(ABC\) is a right angle?

\(\overrightarrow{AC}=\langle-3,2,-6\rangle,\ \overrightarrow{AB}=\langle-6,-3,2\rangle\), and \(\overrightarrow{AC}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=18-6-12=0\), so the angle at \(A\) is a right angle.

Drag to turn the triangle. In most views the right angle doesn’t look like one; the dot product doesn’t care.

The diagonal of a cube

Classic 18.02

What angle does the long diagonal of a cube make with an edge?

Unit cube, diagonal \(\mathbf D=\langle1,1,1\rangle\), edge \(\mathbf E=\langle1,0,0\rangle\):

\[\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf E}{|\mathbf D||\mathbf E|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3\cdot1}\;\Rightarrow\;\theta=\arccos\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\approx54.7^\circ.\]

Method: choose coordinates cleverly, write the two vectors, compute. No trigonometry in 3D needed.

The dot product is a weighted total

IE application

  • Revenue of plan \(\mathbf x\) at prices \(\mathbf p\): \(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x=\sum p_ix_i\).
  • Resource use: if product \(i\) needs \(a_i\) machine-hours, hours used \(=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf x\); the capacity constraint is \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf x\le b\). Every LP constraint is a dot product.
  • Weighted score in multi-criteria decisions: \(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf s\).
  • Similarity of two demand profiles: \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf d_1\cdot\mathbf d_2}{|\mathbf d_1||\mathbf d_2|}\) (“cosine similarity”).

Component of a vector in a direction

Let \(\mathbf u\) be a unit vector.

Scalar component

\[\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf A=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf u=|\mathbf A|\cos\theta\]

A number, and it can be negative.

It is the length of the shadow of \(\mathbf A\) on the line of \(\mathbf u\), with a sign. For a general direction \(\mathbf B\), first make it unit, \(\mathbf u=\mathbf B/|\mathbf B|\):

\[\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf B}\mathbf A=\frac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B}{|\mathbf B|}.\]

Green shadow: positive component. Red shadow: negative.

Projection, the vector version

Orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf A\) onto \(\mathbf B\)

\[\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf B}\mathbf A=(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf u)\,\mathbf u=\frac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B}{|\mathbf B|^2}\,\mathbf B\]

Decomposition into parallel and perpendicular parts:

\[\mathbf A=\underbrace{\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf B}\mathbf A}_{\parallel\mathbf B}+\underbrace{\left(\mathbf A-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf B}\mathbf A\right)}_{\perp\mathbf B}\]

MIT notes S4

\(\mathbf A=\hat\imath+2\hat\jmath,\ \mathbf B=3\hat\imath+4\hat\jmath\). Then \(\mathbf u=\langle\frac35,\frac45\rangle\), component \(\frac{11}5\), projection \(\langle\frac{33}{25},\frac{44}{25}\rangle\), perpendicular part \(\langle-\frac8{25},\frac6{25}\rangle\). Check: \(\langle-8,6\rangle\cdot\langle3,4\rangle=0\). ✓

Forces on an inclined plane

MIT S4 lecture example

A crate of weight \(W\) rests on a ramp inclined at \(\alpha\). Decompose the weight into a part along the ramp and a part into the ramp.

\(\mathbf W=\langle0,-W\rangle\). Unit vectors along and into the ramp: \(\mathbf u_\parallel=\langle\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha\rangle,\ \mathbf u_\perp=\langle\sin\alpha,-\cos\alpha\rangle\).

\[\mathbf W\cdot\mathbf u_\parallel=-W\sin\alpha,\qquad\mathbf W\cdot\mathbf u_\perp=W\cos\alpha.\]

So \(\mathbf W=(-W\sin\alpha)\,\mathbf u_\parallel+(W\cos\alpha)\,\mathbf u_\perp\): sliding force \(W\sin\alpha\), ramp reaction \(W\cos\alpha\).

Same recipe in IE: split a conveyor’s motion into “along” and “across” the line; split a data vector into “trend” and “deviation”.

Drag the top of the ramp, or use the slider.

Distance, spheres and equations by vectors

  • Distance between \(P\) and \(Q\): \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\).
  • Sphere with centre \(C\) and radius \(r\): all \(X\) with \(|\overrightarrow{CX}|=r\), i.e. \((x-c_1)^2+(y-c_2)^2+(z-c_3)^2=r^2\).
  • Perpendicular bisector in the plane of \(P\) and \(Q\): \(\overrightarrow{PX}\cdot\overrightarrow{PQ}=\tfrac12|\overrightarrow{PQ}|^2\).

Example

Which points are equidistant from \(P=(1,0,0)\) and \(Q=(0,2,0)\)?

Set \(|\overrightarrow{PX}|^2=|\overrightarrow{QX}|^2\): \[(x-1)^2+y^2+z^2=x^2+(y-2)^2+z^2\Rightarrow-2x+1=-4y+4\Rightarrow 2x-4y+3=0,\] a plane.

Preview: next week a normal vector \(\mathbf n\) and a point \(P_0\) give a plane by \(\mathbf n\cdot\overrightarrow{P_0X}=0\).

This is the slice \(z=0\). The circles are equal distances: where they meet, \(X\) is equidistant.

Vector proofs: the parallelogram

MIT S1, Problem 3

Prove that the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other.

  1. Sides \(\mathbf A,\mathbf B\) from vertex \(O\). Diagonals: \(\mathbf A+\mathbf B\) (from \(O\)) and \(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\) (from the head of \(\mathbf B\)).
  2. Midpoint of the first diagonal: \(\tfrac12(\mathbf A+\mathbf B)\).
  3. Midpoint of the second: start at \(\mathbf B\), go halfway along \(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\): \[\mathbf B+\tfrac12(\mathbf A-\mathbf B)=\tfrac12(\mathbf A+\mathbf B).\]
  4. Same point, so the diagonals cut each other in half. \(\square\)

The figure follows the proof: each step adds what it describes.

Vector proofs: the rhombus

MIT S3, Problem 3

Show that the diagonals of a parallelogram are perpendicular if and only if it is a rhombus (all sides equal).

Diagonals \(\mathbf A+\mathbf B\) and \(\mathbf A-\mathbf B\). Compute the dot product:

\[(\mathbf A+\mathbf B)\cdot(\mathbf A-\mathbf B)=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A-\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B+\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf A-\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf B=|\mathbf A|^2-|\mathbf B|^2.\] \[\text{diagonals}\perp\iff|\mathbf A|^2-|\mathbf B|^2=0\iff|\mathbf A|=|\mathbf B|\iff\text{rhombus}.\ \square\]

Two tools do all the work

\(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A=|\mathbf A|^2\) and \(\mathbf X\perp\mathbf Y\iff\mathbf X\cdot\mathbf Y=0\).

Try to make the diagonals perpendicular without equal sides. You can’t.

A right angle with a parameter

MIT S3, Problem 2

\(P=(a,1,-1),\ Q=(0,1,1),\ R=(a,-1,3)\). For which \(a\) is the angle \(PQR\) (at \(Q\)) a right angle?

Vectors from \(Q\): \(\overrightarrow{QP}=\langle a,0,-2\rangle,\ \overrightarrow{QR}=\langle a,-2,2\rangle\).

\[\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot\overrightarrow{QR}=a^2+0-4=0\;\Rightarrow\;a=\pm2.\]

Common mistakes

  • Using \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) and \(\overrightarrow{QR}\): they do not share the vertex \(Q\) as tail. The sign flips but the zero condition is the same; still, be consistent.
  • Forgetting that “angle at \(Q\)” means both vectors start at \(Q\).
